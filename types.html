The Thirteen Types

Every one has a name. Nearly every one has a real recording you can press. Most have several, so press again and you'll get a different one.

Before you start These all play out loud. Headphones might be wise, depending where you are. Press Esc to stop everything instantly.

The list

The most common one

The Sputterer

Comes out in bits. Stops, starts, stops again. You'll think it has finished at least twice before it actually has, which is why people stand up too early. Very common after fizzy drinks.

The dangerous one

Silent But Deadly

Everything relaxes, so the gas slides out smoothly instead of flapping anything. No vibration, no sound. The catch is that a slow, quiet exit means the gas sat around longer — and sitting around is exactly what lets the smelly part build up. So the warning noise switches itself off precisely when you'd want it most. This is the central unfairness of the human body.

No recording — obviously Every other one here has a real recording. This one can't. There's no sound to record. The play button does nothing, and that is the joke.

A failed clench

The Squeaker

This is what happens when you try to hold one in and only half succeed. Squeezing makes the gap tiny, and a tiny gap vibrates fast — which is why it comes out as a squeal instead of a rumble. The giveaway is that it goes up in pitch: as you run out of gas you squeeze harder, and it climbs.

Note: the only one where trying harder makes it worse.

The loud one

The Ripper

Starts instantly, loud and messy. It happens when a lot of pressure meets a seal that gives way all at once instead of gradually. The tearing sound comes from the volume flickering about twenty times a second — too fast to hear as a note, so your ear reads it as roughness. Everyone assumes something has ripped. Nothing has. Probably.

Comes in a burst

The Machine Gun

A fast run of separate pops, usually eight to fourteen of them. What makes it different from the Sputterer is rhythm — the Sputterer is chaotic, this one has a beat. Once it starts there's no known way to stop it halfway through.

Note: very common while sitting down, as your weight shifts in stages.

Also called the crop duster

The Ghost

A quiet one released while walking, so instead of leaving a cloud you leave a trail. By the time anyone can smell it you're somewhere else entirely, and the blame lands on whoever stayed put. Morally indefensible. Tactically perfect.

Note: the only one defined by how you were moving rather than how it sounded.

A warning, not a sound

The Wet One

Muffled and gurgling, because there's liquid involved. The wetness drags the pitch down and smears the sound out, which is what makes the squelch.

This one is a warning Every other type on this page is a description. This one is an instruction: stop what you're doing, find a toilet, reconsider your afternoon.

The Crescendo

Starts quiet and low, then climbs in both pitch and volume. It rises because you can hear it happening and start clenching partway through — and clenching raises the pitch. A rare case where trying to fix something makes it audibly, measurably worse in real time, while everyone listens.

Two things at once

The Sneeze-Fart

A sneeze spikes the pressure inside you, and if everything below is briefly off guard, a short loud one comes out at the same instant. The timing is the whole point — and also your only defence, since the sneeze partly covers it. Which is why it's more deniable than its volume deserves.

Note: the same thing happens with laughing and coughing.

The useful one

The Chair Creak

Defined not by how it's made but by how it's heard. Steady, smooth, mid-pitched — it sounds almost exactly like leather, vinyl, or a cheap office chair wheel. People genuinely can't tell the difference. This is the most useful one there is, and the only one you can set up in advance by picking the right chair.

Note: shift your weight straight afterwards. Sell it.

The long one

The Marathon

Anything over five seconds without stopping. It needs a lot of gas and a gap that stays half open the whole way — never shutting, never quite letting go. People listening go through amusement, then disbelief, then genuine concern, at about two-second intervals.

The smallest one

The Pop

A single blip, about a fifth of a second, with nothing after it. It's over before your ears have worked out where it came from, so people often aren't sure it happened at all. Do not help them decide.

Note: often the only audible part of a much bigger silent one. The Pop is the doorbell.

The absolute maximum

The Afterburner

Maximum pressure, maximum everything, pushed so far that the sound stops being a note and turns into pure noise. Usually follows a lot of beans plus a long hold. Gets close to the volume of a passing motorbike. People claim nearby furniture vibrates, though nobody has ever checked properly.

Still don't set it on fire This is the one people are most tempted to try it with. The flame can travel back the wrong way. Please don't.

Notice the pattern

The quiet ones smell the worst

Go back through the list and compare the loudness bar against the smell bar. They run in opposite directions nearly every time. The loudest ones are fairly harmless. The silent ones are the problem.

So the noise is really a warning system — one that works perfectly, except in exactly the cases where you'd actually want it.

Where the sounds came from

Every sound here is a real recording. Nothing is fake or computer-made.

They come from Wikimedia Commons, where people have uploaded them for anyone to use. The biggest source is a five-minute recording released by Finland's national broadcaster, described simply as "different kind of real farts", which we chopped into 62 separate ones.

That left 83 to choose from. Rather than picking by ear, we measured each one — how long, how high, how rough, how many bursts — and matched them to whichever type they fitted best.

Twelve of the thirteen found a match. The only one that didn't is Silent But Deadly, for reasons that should be obvious.

If you want to reuse them The recordings belong to the people who made them, shared under Creative Commons licences — mostly CC BY and CC BY-SA, with one in the public domain. Thanks to Zache (who uploaded the Finnish broadcast archive), Horst Frank, Prononcateur, natalie and Clément Girardot-Constand. Everything else on this site is free for anyone.
