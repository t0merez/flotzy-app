Original Research · Systematic Flatology

A Typology of Flatus

Thirteen acoustically distinct classes, formally described, named, measured, and — for the first time in the literature — matched to real recordings by their acoustics rather than by ear.

Department of Systematics · Flotzy Institute
Specimens collected opportunistically. Consent was not obtained; it rarely is.

Abstract

Nobody has ever sorted farts properly. We have. We sort them by sound, not by smell, because smell has nothing to do with anything you can hear (see Part I). Thirteen types are described. Each is given a binomial name and a set of measured parameters. Twelve are illustrated with a real recording, drawn from freely licensed archives and matched to its class by measured acoustics — duration, fundamental frequency, spectral centroid, burst structure and pitch contour — rather than by ear. The last one is silent by definition and has no audio, for the obvious reason.

Playback notice. Every recording plays through your speakers. Consider headphones if your surroundings include colleagues, a library, or anyone who has already asked you to stop. Press Esc to silence everything at once.

How to read an entry

F₀ — pitch. How high or low it is, in hertz. Low numbers rumble, high numbers squeak.

SPL — loudness, in decibels at one metre. A whisper is 30, talking is 60, a vacuum cleaner is 75.

Odour. How much it smells, out of ten. Has nothing to do with loudness. Never has anything to do with loudness.

Deniability. Your odds, out of ten, of blaming it on a chair, a door, a pipe, or a dog.

Moisture risk. The chance that something else comes too. Above 6, stop what you are doing and find a toilet.

Waveform. The peak envelope of the actual recording, measured at 150 points.

Measured or estimated? Duration, pitch and brightness are measured from the real recording. Loudness, odour, deniability and moisture can't be — those are estimates.

Systematic descriptions

Plate I · FL-001

The Sputterer

Flatus staccato

The most common type by far. The seal keeps opening and shutting under uneven pressure, so instead of one clean note you get a broken, stuttering run. You will think it has finished at least twice before it actually has. Very common after fizzy drinks, where the gas arrives in uneven lumps.

Field note: the defining feature is not the sound but the false ending. Do not stand up.

Plate II · FL-002

Silent But Deadly

Silentium letale

The dangerous one. Everything is relaxed, so the gas slides out smoothly instead of flapping anything — no vibration, no sound. The catch: a slow release means the gas sat in the gut longer, and sitting longer is exactly what lets the smelly sulfur build up. So the warning noise switches itself off precisely when you need it most. This is the central unfairness of the human body.

Field note: the audio exemplar is near-inaudible by design. This is not a bug. Raise your volume at your own considerable risk.

No recording Every other class on this page is illustrated with a real recording. This one cannot be. A silent emission produces no acoustic signal to record, so the play control is inert and the waveform is flat. The absence is the specimen.

Plate III · FL-003

The Squeaker

Stridor angustus

This is what a failed clench sounds like. Squeezing hard makes the gap tiny, and a tiny gap vibrates fast — up in the 300–450 Hz range, way above everything else. Hence the squeal. The giveaway is that the pitch rises: as you run out of gas you clench harder, and it goes up instead of down.

Field note: the only class in which increased effort produces a worse outcome. Consider surrender.

Plate IV · FL-003

The Ripper

Lacerator textilis

Starts instantly, loud, and messy. It happens when a lot of pressure meets a seal that gives way all at once rather than gradually — nothing really vibrates, it just gets overwhelmed. The tearing sound comes from the volume flickering 20–30 times a second, which is too fast to hear as a pitch, so your ear reads it as roughness instead.

Field note: the species name refers to the widespread belief that trousers have been damaged. They have not. Probably.

Plate V · FL-004

The Machine Gun

Rapidus multiplex

A fast run of separate pops — usually eight to fourteen of them, about five to eight per second. What separates it from the Sputterer (FL-001) is rhythm: the Sputterer is chaotic, this one has a beat. Once it starts there is no known way to stop it partway through.

Field note: commonly reported during the act of sitting down, when posture change modulates abdominal pressure in stages.

Plate VI · FL-005

The Ghost (“Crop Duster”)

Spectrum ambulans

A silent one released while walking, so instead of one cloud you leave a trail. By the time anyone can smell it you are somewhere else, and the blame lands on whoever stayed put. Morally indefensible. Tactically perfect. The only type defined by how the person was moving rather than by how it sounded.

Field note: optimal walking speed is 1.2 m/s. Faster produces turbulent mixing and dilution; slower risks self-contamination.

The quietest specimen we found A Ghost is nearly silent, so we matched it to the dullest recording in the whole corpus: spectral centroid 249 Hz, against 716 Hz for the next dullest. It is a low, muffled thing you would miss in a noisy room — which is the point.

Plate VII · FL-007

The Wet One

Periculum humidum

Muffled and gurgling, because there is liquid involved. The wetness drags the pitch down and smears the sound out, which is what makes the squelch. This is the only type on this page that is a warning rather than a description. Everyone agrees on what to do: stop, find a toilet, reconsider.

Category 1 alert Any specimen exhibiting a moisture index above 6.0 must be treated as a failed containment event in progress. Do not attempt amplification (Part IV(a)). Do not attempt anything.

Plate VIII · FL-006

The Crescendo

Crescendo terribilis

Starts quiet and low, then climbs in both pitch and volume over two to four seconds. It rises because you hear it happening and start clenching partway through — and clenching raises the pitch. A rare case where trying to fix something makes it audibly, measurably worse in real time.

Field note: psychologically the most distressing class, as the victim can hear it getting worse and can do nothing.

Plate IX · FL-007

The Sneeze-Fart

Sternuto-flatus

Two things at once, neither on purpose. A sneeze spikes the pressure in your abdomen, and if everything below is briefly off guard, a short loud one comes out at the same instant. The timing is the whole point — and also your only defence, since the sneeze partly covers it. That is why it is more deniable than its volume suggests.

Field note: the same mechanism explains the laugh-fart and the cough-fart, which we treat as subspecies rather than separate classes.

Plate X · FL-008

The Chair Creak

Ambiguitas sellae

Defined not by how it is made but by how it is heard. In the 180–260 Hz range, steady and smooth, it sounds almost exactly like leather, vinyl or a cheap office castor. People genuinely cannot tell. This is the most useful type there is, and the only one you can deliberately set up in advance by picking the right chair — see substrate selection.

Field note: shift your weight immediately afterward. Sell it.

Plate XI · FL-009

The Marathon

Longitudo aeterna

Defined only by length: anything over five seconds without stopping. It needs a lot of gas and a seal that stays half-open the whole way — never shutting, never fully letting go. The tone drifts as the pressure drops. Bystanders reliably pass through amusement, then disbelief, then genuine concern, at roughly two-second intervals.

Field note: the exemplar runs 6.5 s. Documented claims beyond two minutes exist but originate entirely from the internet and are treated as folklore.

Plate XII · FL-010

The Pop

Punctum breve

The smallest one that counts: a single blip of a tenth to a fifth of a second, with nothing after it. Basically one open-and-shut. It is over before your ears have worked out where it came from, so witnesses often aren't sure it happened. Do not help them decide.

Field note: often the audible portion of a much larger silent emission. The Pop is the doorbell; FL-002 is what is standing outside.

Plate XIII · FL-011

The Afterburner

Ignis posterior

The ceiling. Maximum pressure, maximum flow, pushed so far past normal that the sound stops being a note and becomes pure noise. It usually follows a lot of beans (see Part III) plus a long hold. Reported loudness gets close to a passing motorbike. People claim nearby furniture vibrates. Nobody has checked properly.

Do not ignite The hydrogen and methane fractions are flammable and the flame front can propagate toward the source. The Institute has received exactly one report on this subject and has no wish to receive a second.

Principal finding Across all thirteen classes, odour index and sound pressure level are inversely correlated (r ≈ −0.62). The quietest specimens are the most dangerous. Loudness is, in effect, an early warning system that fires reliably in exactly the cases where no warning is needed.

Provenance, method and licences

Every audible specimen on this site is a real recording. Nothing is synthesised. Candidate audio was gathered from Wikimedia Commons — including a 4 min 50 s archive compilation released by the Finnish public broadcaster YLE, which we segmented into 62 discrete events by energy gating.

Each of the 83 resulting candidates was then measured for duration, fundamental frequency (autocorrelation with interior peak-picking after a 1.2 kHz low-pass), spectral centroid, spectral flatness, burst count and regularity, attack time, crest factor and pitch contour.

Classes were assigned by scoring every candidate against the criteria in the dichotomous key and solving a global one-to-one assignment, so the taxonomy did the classifying rather than our ears. Twelve of thirteen classes found a credible match.

Two did not, and are presented without audio: FL-002 Silent But Deadly and FL-006 The Ghost. No recording of a silent fart can exist. That is, after all, the entire point of them.
