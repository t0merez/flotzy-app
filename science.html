Multi-Medium Simulation

A Lagrangian particle model of flatal propagation, solved live in your browser across nine physical media and rendered five different ways — including two in which the emission makes no sound at all, and one in which it makes considerably more.

Department of Computational Flatodynamics · Flotzy Institute
See also the subordinate report: IV(a) · Acoustic Amplification & Resonance Engineering

Abstract

Flatus is a buoyant, warm, multi-component gas jet released into a stationary medium. Its subsequent behaviour is governed almost entirely by three properties of that medium — density, viscosity and speed of sound — and by two of the source: aperture diameter and charge volume. We implement a particle-advection model with drag, buoyancy and stochastic diffusion, and expose it as an interactive instrument. The principal finding is that the medium matters far more than the emitter. The same 180 mL charge that disperses across two metres in air travels under 40 cm in honey, sinks rather than rises in sulfur hexafluoride, and in vacuum expands spherically and silently forever.

Model: Lagrangian parcel advection with linear (Stokes-regime) drag, buoyancy proportional to parcel–ambient temperature difference, and Gaussian diffusion. Acoustic wavefronts are drawn at slow motion so that media can be compared visually; true arrival times appear in the readouts. Audio is the real recording for the selected class, retuned and filtered by the medium.

Reading the instrument

Two readouts do most of the explanatory work.

Reynolds number — the ratio of inertial to viscous forces, Re = ρvd/µ — decides whether the emission behaves as a jet or as a slow bleed. Below about 10 the flow is creeping: viscosity dominates completely and the gas barely moves. Between 10 and 2300 it is laminar, holding together as a coherent stream. Above 4000 it is turbulent, breaking into eddies that mix aggressively with the surroundings. In air a typical emission sits around Re ≈ 1600 — laminar at the aperture, turbulent within about 20 cm.

Olfactory detection — the moment concentration at the two-metre mark crosses threshold. Compare this to sound at 2 m and the central asymmetry of the field becomes obvious: the acoustic warning arrives in about six milliseconds, the gas in several seconds. That interval is the only thing standing between a bystander and the consequences, and the classes that make no sound remove it entirely.

Try this. Select Vacuum and the Acoustic view. The wavefront display goes blank. There is no medium, so there is no wave. The particle plume, meanwhile, expands in every direction and never slows down, because there is nothing to slow it.

The medium library

Nine media, ordered by how badly they treat the emitter.

Medium 1 · Reference

Standard Air

ρ 1.20 kg/m³ · µ 1.81×10⁻⁵ Pa·s · c 343 m/s

The baseline against which everything else is anomalous. A warm plume at 37 °C is roughly 5% less dense than 21 °C room air, giving gentle buoyancy — the plume rises at around 15 cm/s while turbulent diffusion broadens it into the familiar cone. Detection at 2 m follows in three to eight seconds depending on ventilation.

Medium 2 · The bath

Fresh Water

ρ 998 kg/m³ · µ 1.00×10⁻³ Pa·s · c 1482 m/s

Density increases 830-fold, so buoyancy dominates absolutely — the gas cannot disperse and instead pinches off into discrete bubbles that rise at 20–30 cm/s. Sound is produced at the surface, on rupture, not at the aperture. Individual bubbles also ring at their Minnaert frequency, f ≈ 3.26/R Hz·m, which puts a 5 mm bubble at about 650 Hz.

Medium 3 · Creeping flow

Honey

ρ 1420 kg/m³ · µ ≈ 10 Pa·s · c 2030 m/s

Viscosity is roughly 550 000 times that of air. Reynolds number collapses to order 1, placing the flow in the Stokes regime where inertia is irrelevant and motion is fully reversible. The emission travels perhaps 30 cm and then simply stops. Acoustically it is almost entirely absorbed within centimetres.

Medium 4 · No medium

Vacuum

ρ 0 · µ 0 · c — (no propagation)

Sound requires a medium; there isn't one. The gas undergoes free molecular expansion — no drag, no buoyancy, no boundary — spreading spherically and forever. By conservation of momentum the operator acquires a velocity change in the opposite direction: for a 180 mL charge at 3 m/s from an 80 kg body, roughly 8 µm/s. It is propulsion. It is extraordinarily bad propulsion.

Medium 5 · Porous solid

Upholstery

Darcy flow · permeability ≪ 1 · absorptive

Flow through a porous solid obeys Darcy's law rather than Navier–Stokes at the macro scale: volumetric flux is proportional to the pressure gradient and inversely proportional to viscosity, scaled by a permeability that is very small. Fabric is simultaneously an acoustic absorber (removing the high frequencies that make an emission identifiable) and a physical reservoir that releases the retained fraction later. This is the entire scientific basis for distrusting other people's sofas.

Medium 6 · Microgravity

Zero-G Cabin

air · g ≈ 0 · no convection

Remove gravity and you remove buoyancy, and with it all natural convection. The plume expands as a symmetric sphere and then remains precisely where it was created, mixing only by diffusion. Crewed spacecraft handle this with continuous forced-air circulation, which is also why astronauts sleep beside a fan: without one they would slowly accumulate a personal atmosphere.

Medium 7 · Cryogenic

Liquid Nitrogen

77 K · ρ 807 kg/m³ · violently boiling

At 77 K the CO₂ fraction desublimates directly to solid and the water vapour flash-freezes, physically removing them from the gas phase. The residual N₂ and H₂ drive vigorous film boiling. The odour problem is solved completely, at a cost that no reasonable person would accept.

Medium 8 · Anti-helium

Sulfur Hexafluoride

ρ 6.16 kg/m³ · c 134 m/s

Five times denser than air, so the plume sinks and pools at floor level rather than rising — an inversion of every intuition in the field. Sound travels at 39% of its speed in air, dropping all cavity resonances by roughly an octave and a half. Select this medium and fire: the same class you just heard in air returns an octave lower.

Medium 9 · The good one

Helium Atmosphere

ρ 0.166 kg/m³ · c 1007 m/s

Sound travels 2.9× faster than in air, raising every resonance by about an octave and a half while the reed frequency itself barely shifts — exactly the mechanism behind the helium voice effect, applied at the opposite end of the digestive tract. The plume also ascends roughly four times faster. This is, by an enormous margin, the funniest entry in the library.

Model limitations This is a two-dimensional particle model with linear drag and no ambient flow field. It does not solve Navier–Stokes, does not resolve turbulence, and does not model the multi-component thermodynamics of a gas mixture with six constituents at different partial pressures. It is intended to be instructive and enjoyable rather than publishable, which — given the subject — was never really in question.
