Abstract

Flatus — from the Latin flatus, "a blowing" — is the terminal expression of gases accumulated within the human gastrointestinal tract. Despite being produced by approximately one hundred percent of the population on a daily basis, the phenomenon remains conspicuously under-theorised outside of the schoolyard. This journal exists to correct that imbalance. We present a unified account of flatal science across four domains: composition, in which we establish that the offending gas is overwhelmingly odourless; taxonomy, in which we classify thirteen acoustically distinct specimens; substrate loading, in which forty-two foodstuffs are ranked by measured gas yield; and propagation, in which we simulate flatal behaviour across nine physical media, including several in which no human should reasonably attempt it.

Keywords: flatus · flatulence · borborygmus · raffinose · hydrogen sulfide · sphincteric acoustics · Helmholtz coupling · plausible deniability

Introduction

The average adult human produces between 0.5 and 2.0 litres of intestinal gas per day and releases it across five to twenty-five discrete events.1 This is not a disorder. It is not a moral failing. It is the unavoidable exhaust of a fermentation reactor that you carry with you at all times and feed several times a day, largely without consulting it.

Two mechanisms supply the gas. The first is aerophagia — swallowed atmospheric air, which arrives already assembled and is mostly nitrogen. The second, and by far the more interesting, is bacterial fermentation: the colonic microbiota metabolising carbohydrates that the small intestine could not break down, and exhaling hydrogen, carbon dioxide and — in roughly one third of the population — methane as a byproduct.2

The central and most counter-intuitive finding of the entire field is this: flatus is approximately 99% odourless. The nitrogen, hydrogen, carbon dioxide, oxygen and methane that constitute the overwhelming bulk of the emission have no smell whatsoever. The reputation of the entire phenomenon rests on a trace fraction — under one percent — of volatile sulfur compounds, principally hydrogen sulfide, methanethiol and dimethyl sulfide.3 The human nose detects hydrogen sulfide at concentrations near 0.5 parts per billion. It is, in effect, a rounding error that has ruined countless elevator rides.

On the word. Hebrew פלוץ (flotz), n., a fart; להפליץ (lehaflitz), v., to produce one. The journal takes its name from the noun, in acknowledgement that the noun is the more studied of the two.

Two consequences follow. First, volume and odour are independent variables. A large, acoustically impressive emission of hydrogen and carbon dioxide may be entirely inoffensive, while a silent 30 mL release rich in hydrogen sulfide can clear a room. This decoupling is the organising principle behind our typology (§ Typology) and our substrate rankings (§ Substrates), both of which score volume and odour separately.

Second, hydrogen and methane are flammable. We note this for completeness and immediately advise against acting on it.4

Exit velocity deserves comment. At roughly 3 metres per second, flatus travels considerably slower than the sound it makes — the acoustic wavefront arrives at a bystander approximately 114 times faster than the gas itself. At two metres' separation this yields a warning interval of about 0.66 seconds. This interval is the entire basis of the tactical literature and is discussed at length in Acoustic Amplification & Resonance Engineering.

The gap. Sound: 343 m/s. Gas: 3 m/s. You will always hear it before you smell it — unless it was silent, in which case the warning system has been deliberately disabled by the operator.

Acoustic principles

Flatus is not, acoustically speaking, the sound of gas. It is the sound of tissue. The external anal sphincter functions as a lip-reed valve — mechanically equivalent to the embouchure of a trumpeter — in which pressurised flow drives two apposed soft-tissue folds into self-sustained oscillation.5 The gas itself is silent. It is merely the working fluid.

Three parameters govern the resulting sound:

Parameter I

Aperture tension

Sets the fundamental frequency. A tight aperture oscillates fast and high — the squeak. A relaxed aperture oscillates slow and low — the rumble. Range observed: 25–450 Hz.

Parameter II

Volumetric flow rate

Sets amplitude. Loudness scales with the pressure differential across the valve, which is why retention increases output — see the retention protocol.

Parameter III

Acoustic coupling

Sets everything else. A bare wooden bench reflects; upholstery absorbs; a bathtub resonates. The same emission can vary by 18 dB depending entirely on furniture.

Methods, ethics and limitations

Methods Figures in this journal are drawn from the published gastroenterological literature where such literature exists, and from confident estimation where it does not. Acoustic parameters are modelled rather than measured. The taxonomy is our own and has been peer-reviewed by three colleagues who found it funny.
Ethics statement No human subjects were formally enrolled. All authors contributed data continuously and involuntarily throughout the study period. The institutional review board was informed and asked us to stop emailing them.
Clinical note Flatulence is normal. Persistent bloating, sudden change in bowel habit, blood, unexplained weight loss or severe pain are not, and warrant an actual physician rather than a satirical website. Nothing here is medical advice.
Limitations The principal limitation of this work is that it is a website about farts. Secondary limitations include the absence of controls, the absence of a control group, the absence of ethics approval, and the authors' evident enthusiasm for the subject matter.
