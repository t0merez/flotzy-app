Abstract

A fart is gas leaving your gut. Everybody makes it, every day, and almost nobody has looked at it properly. This journal does.

We cover four things. What it is made of — and why 99% of it has no smell at all. What kinds there are — thirteen types, each with a real recording. What causes it — 42 foods, ranked. How it travels — a simulation you can run in air, water, honey and six other things you should not try.

Introduction

A normal adult makes half a litre to two litres of gut gas a day, and lets it out five to twenty-five times.1 That is not a problem or a personal failing. It is the exhaust of a fermentation tank you carry around and feed several times a day without asking it first.

The gas comes from two places. Some is air you swallowed — mostly nitrogen, which arrives ready-made. The rest is made by gut bacteria eating the carbohydrates your small intestine could not. They breathe out hydrogen, carbon dioxide, and — in about a third of people — methane.2

Here is the surprising part: 99% of a fart has no smell. Nitrogen, hydrogen, carbon dioxide, oxygen and methane are all odourless. The entire reputation of the thing rests on less than 1% — a trace of sulfur compounds, mainly hydrogen sulfide.3 Your nose can detect hydrogen sulfide at about 0.5 parts per billion. It is a rounding error that has ruined a lot of lift journeys.

On the word. Hebrew פלוץ (flotz), n., a fart; להפליץ (lehaflitz), v., to produce one. The journal takes its name from the noun, in acknowledgement that the noun is the more studied of the two.

That exit speed is worth a moment. At about 3 metres per second, the gas moves far slower than its own sound — the noise reaches a bystander roughly 114 times sooner than the smell does. Two metres away, that is about 0.66 seconds of warning. Everything tactical about this subject comes down to those 0.66 seconds.

Acoustic principles

The sound is not the gas. It is skin. The sphincter works like a trumpeter's lips: pressure pushes through a narrow gap and makes the soft edges flap.5 The gas is silent — it is only what does the pushing.

Three things decide what it sounds like:

Parameter I

Aperture tension

Sets the pitch. Tight flaps fast and high — the squeak. Loose flaps slow and low — the rumble. Real range: 25–450 Hz.

Parameter II

Volumetric flow rate

Sets the volume. More pressure means more noise, which is why holding it in makes it louder — see amplification.

Parameter III

Acoustic coupling

Sets everything else. Wood reflects, cushions absorb, a bathtub rings. The same fart can vary by 18 dB based purely on what you are sitting on.

Methods, ethics and limitations

Methods Figures in this journal are drawn from the published gastroenterological literature where such literature exists, and from confident estimation where it does not. Acoustic parameters are modelled rather than measured. The taxonomy is our own and has been peer-reviewed by three colleagues who found it funny.
Ethics statement No human subjects were formally enrolled. All authors contributed data continuously and involuntarily throughout the study period. The institutional review board was informed and asked us to stop emailing them.
Clinical note Flatulence is normal. Persistent bloating, sudden change in bowel habit, blood, unexplained weight loss or severe pain are not, and warrant an actual physician rather than a satirical website. Nothing here is medical advice.
Limitations The principal limitation of this work is that it is a website about farts. Secondary limitations include the absence of controls, the absence of a control group, the absence of ethics approval, and the authors' evident enthusiasm for the subject matter.
